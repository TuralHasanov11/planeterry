# Who Made These Tiny Footprints?

**Episode:** 003  
**Total duration:** 30 seconds  
**Scenes:** 6  
**Format:** YouTube Short (vertical 9:16)  
**Target audience:** Children aged 4–8  
**Category:** Animal or nature discovery  
**Discovery:** Rabbits move by hopping. When a rabbit bounds, its larger hind feet usually land ahead of its smaller front feet, creating a distinctive track pattern.

## Story overview

While exploring a forest trail, Max discovers a set of tiny footprints pressed into a soft patch of mud. The friends follow the tracks, but Robo gets himself turned around and accidentally follows his own bootprints. Luna studies the pattern and notices that the larger prints appear ahead of the smaller ones. The trail leads to a little rabbit beside a bush. The friends realize that the tracks likely belong to the rabbit and learn that its strong hind legs help it hop.

The story is observational and gentle: the friends do not touch, chase, or disturb the rabbit.

## Continuity and production notes

- Keep the entire story on one forest exploration trail so the environment can be reused across scenes.
- Maintain the same damp patch of earth and track direction in Scenes 1–5.
- Distinguish the rabbit's small animal tracks from Robo's larger mechanical bootprints.
- The rabbit track pattern should be shown as two smaller front-foot impressions behind two larger hind-foot impressions in the direction of travel.
- Do not claim that tracks alone prove the animal's identity; the rabbit's appearance provides the visual confirmation.
- Do not show the friends touching, feeding, chasing, or frightening the rabbit.
- Keep the rabbit's movement gentle and natural. The short hop is a visual demonstration, not a full scientific time-lapse.
- Keep dialogue warm, short, and easy for children aged 4–8 to understand.
- Use gentle forest ambience and playful sound effects; avoid startling sounds.
- Maintain character reference consistency in every generated image and video.
